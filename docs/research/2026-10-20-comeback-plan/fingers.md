# Climbing finger injuries: evidence review for the CruxCoach rehab program

Research report for the CruxCoach injury-rehabilitation feature (run `injury-rehab-20261020`). Date: 2026-10-20.
Scope: diagnosis/triage, conservative treatment, rehab loading, and return-to-climbing for climbing finger injuries,
plus a critical review of the example plan the owner supplied (`../example-plan.md`).

## How to read this report

**Evidence-level tags** (attached to every key statement):

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[SR]** | Systematic review / meta-analysis / scoping review |
| **[RCT]** | Randomised (incl. crossover) controlled trial |
| **[Cohort]** | Prospective or retrospective cohort, cross-sectional or diagnostic-accuracy study with a defined sample |
| **[CS]** | Case series or case report |
| **[Lab]** | Cadaver, biomechanical, in-vitro or acute-physiology study (mechanistic, not clinical outcome) |
| **[Review]** | Narrative review / clinical commentary by clinician-researchers (expert synthesis) |
| **[Consensus]** | Formal consensus (Delphi etc.). **None exists yet for climbing finger rehab** (one Delphi is in progress, see §5) |
| **[EO]** | Expert/practitioner opinion (clinic blogs, coaching material), clearly not peer-reviewed evidence |

**Access transparency.** Full text was read for: Schöffl et al. 2020 (MLTJ), Larsson et al. 2022 (SR), Bosco et al. 2022,
Crowley 2012, Mergoum et al. 2025, Shaw et al. 2017, Gilmore et al. 2024, Vagy 2023, Jaini et al. 2026, Leggit & Meko 2006,
Schweizer 2012, and the practitioner pages (Hooper's Beta, The Climbing Doctor). The rest were assessed from abstracts or
from verified secondary reproductions. Paywalled items include Schöffl 2003/2006 (original grading table), Ehiogu et al.
2023, Cooper & LaStayo 2020, Meyers et al. 2020, Manjunathan & Liem 2026 and Bellomo et al. 2024. These are flagged where used.
I could not access *One Move Too Many* (Hochholzer & Schöffl) directly. Its clinical content is largely published in the
Schöffl papers cited here. I found no accessible written pulley-rehab protocol by Eva López or Tyler Nelson, so neither is cited.

**Bottom line on evidence quality:** the diagnosis and the broad treatment pathway for pulley injuries rest on
large clinical case series from specialised climbing clinics (mainly Bamberg/Schöffl and Balgrist/Schweizer) plus
good diagnostic-accuracy data for ultrasound. **Rehab loading parameters (sets, reps, % loads, pain thresholds,
return-to-climb criteria) have no climbing-specific trial evidence at all.** They are extrapolated from tendon/ligament
science and from practitioner protocols. An app should present them as "a structured, conservative starting point", not as proven.

## 1. Injury types and presentation

### 1.1 Anatomy and load: the facts that drive everything else

- **Crimping loads the pulleys far more than open-hand grips.** A biomechanical model estimated A2 pulley force to be
  **36× lower in the slope/open grip than in the crimp, but A4 force only 4× lower**. The FDP:FDS tension ratio is 1.75:1 in
  crimp vs 0.88:1 in slope (Vigouroux et al. 2006) **[Lab]**. *App implication:* open-hand loading strongly unloads A2 but
  still loads A4 substantially, so an A4 injury can hurt in open-hand positions too.
- In vivo, the A2 pulley carried about **3× the fingertip force** in crimp (up to 116 N measured). Physiological bowstringing at the
  distal A2 edge rose by **0.6 mm (+30%) during a warm-up** (Schweizer 2001) **[Lab]**. For this reason a graded warm-up of
  **3–4 routes of ~40 moves or 8–12 boulders** is recommended before hard crimping (Schweizer 2012; Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Review]**.
- Pulleys are most at risk under **eccentric load**, i.e. when the hand is forced open on a hold, as in a foot slip (Miro et al. 2021) **[Review]**.
- Cadaver pulley rupture forces ranged 153–569 N, and younger specimens (20–47 y) failed at 482–516 N (cited in Larsson et al. 2022) **[Lab]**.

### 1.2 Pulley injuries (A2/A3/A4 strains, partial and complete ruptures, multiple ruptures)

**Epidemiology.**
- In 604 injured climbers (1998–2001), pulley injuries made up 20%, tenosynovitis 7% and joint-capsule injuries 6.1%. Of the
  122 pulley injuries, 48 were strains and 74 ruptures (90.5% single, 9.5% multiple). By grade: **39% I, 25% II, 30% III, 6% IV**
  (Schöffl et al. 2003) **[Cohort/CS]**.
- In 911 injuries (2009–2012), fingers accounted for 52% and pulley injuries were the most frequent finger injury, with
  **A4 now more often injured than A2** (Schöffl et al. 2015) **[CS]**.
- In 2017–18, pulley injury was still the most frequent diagnosis, followed by tenosynovitis and finger-joint capsulitis.
  Lumbrical injuries were in the top 10 (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Cohort]**.
- A 2026 review puts pulley injuries at "up to 20% of all climbing injuries" (Manjunathan & Liem 2026, abstract) **[Review]**.

**Schöffl grading** (Schöffl et al. 2003; Schöffl & Schöffl 2006; as reproduced in Bosco et al. 2022 and Crowley 2012) **[CS → widely adopted]**:

| Grade | Lesion |
|---|---|
| I | Pulley strain / slight tear (tendon–bone distance < 2 mm on US/MRI) |
| II | Complete A4 rupture **or** partial A2/A3 rupture |
| III | Complete A2 or A3 rupture |
| IV | Multiple ruptures (A2+A3, A2+A3+A4), **or** a single A2/A3 rupture combined with lumbrical or collateral-ligament injury |

Newer nuance:
- A prospective Balgrist series of 34 A2 ruptures found **none complete**. The median rupture extent was **63.6%**, always tearing from
  distal to proximal, and only 5.9% were combined A2–A4. The authors simplified their own grading as a result (Mohn et al. 2026) **[CS]**.
- Cadaver work suggests A2/A4 can be opened up to ~25% without functional deficit (cited in Bosco et al. 2022) **[Lab]**.
- Grade III sits in a "grey zone" where both conservative and surgical treatment can give good return to sport (Bosco et al. 2022) **[Review]**.

**Typical presentation.**
- **Mechanism:** a sudden load on a crimp, especially a **foot slip / shock load** or a hand forced open, or progressive overload
  over a session or weeks. A **"pop"/snapping sound** suggests rupture (Schöffl et al. 2020; Crowley 2012) **[Review]**. A pop is
  not diagnostic: it can also be joint cavitation, and a full tear can be surprisingly painless (Hooper, A2 manual) **[EO]**.
- **Location:**
  - A2: palm side of the proximal phalanx (base of the finger).
  - A3: palm side over the PIP joint.
  - A4: palm side of the middle phalanx.
  - The fingers involved were not reliably reported in the sources I could access, so the app should not assume a typical finger.
- **Signs:**
  - Local tenderness and swelling, sometimes a haematoma.
  - Pain on resisted flexion, worse in half/full crimp than open hand.
  - "Painful bowstringing palpable during resisted flexion" in ruptures (Schöffl et al. 2020; Mergoum et al. 2025) **[Review]**.
- **Bowstringing caveat:** in cadavers, **isolated A2 or A4 rupture produced no detectable bowstringing**. Subtle bowstringing
  appeared only after two consecutive pulleys had failed, and obvious bowstringing needed A2+A3+A4 (Marco et al. 1998) **[Lab]**.
  So **visible bowstringing means a multiple-pulley (grade IV-type) injury**, and the absence of bowstringing does **not**
  exclude a rupture.

**Clinical (non-imaging) severity schema.** Cooper & LaStayo 2020 proposed a schema for A2 strain. The text below is taken
from its reproduction on The Climbing Doctor, as the original is paywalled **[EO/proposal]**:

| Domain | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain in daily life | 0/10, not limiting | 3–5/10, not limiting | ≥5/10, limits activity |
| Pain climbing | ≤2/10 after climbing; only crimp painful | ≥5/10, limits all grips | >5/10, severely limits |
| Active ROM | pain at end-range flexion, no loss | end-range pain, ≤25% ROM loss | pain and >50% ROM loss |
| Resisted grip tests | sloper 0, half crimp ≤2, full crimp ≤2 /10 | sloper ≤2, half 2–5, full 6–8 /10 | pain and weakness in any grip |
| Palpation | pain only with maximal (nail-blanching) pressure | pain with moderate pressure | pain with minimal pressure |

The Climbing Doctor notes that **no correlation between this clinical grading and ultrasound grade has been shown** **[EO]**.
For an app this is the most usable published structure for self-report triage, but it is unvalidated.

### 1.3 Flexor tenosynovitis
- Tenosynovitis is the most important differential of pulley injury and the most frequent finger overuse syndrome. It is an
  inflammation of the tendon sheath, not of the tendon (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Review]**.
- **Presentation:** pain and sometimes mild swelling along the palm side of the finger, in the same area as pulley pain,
  often extending into the palm or forearm. Onset may be acute or chronic.
- **Diagnosis:** ultrasound shows a fluid "halo" around the tendon. Climbers normally carry extra sheath fluid, so the finding is
  compared with the same finger on the other hand.
- **Course:** in 65 climbers, mean symptom duration was **30.5 weeks (range 1–120)**. 91% were treated with climbing-load
  reduction, all resumed climbing, and 75% regained or exceeded their level. Higher daily-life pain and a grade above 7b predicted a
  worse or longer course (Mohn et al. 2022) **[Cohort, retrospective]**.
- **After pulley rupture:** a capsized pulley stump can cause chronic tenosynovitis (the "flap irritation phenomenon"), sometimes
  needing surgical removal (Schöffl et al. 2011) **[Lab + 2 cases]**.

### 1.4 Lumbrical tears/strains ("lumbrical shift syndrome")
- **Mechanism:** one or two fingers extended while the neighbours are strongly flexed, as in a mono/two-finger pocket or a
  ring/little finger in a pocket. The deep-flexor tendons then shift against each other (the quadriga effect) and shear the
  bipennate lumbrical between them (Schweizer 2003 **[CS, n=3]**; Schöffl et al. 2020 **[Review]**). The mixed position raises
  maximal force by up to 50% (Schöffl et al. 2020).
- **Typical sign:** pain in the palm or at the base of the fingers **only when one finger is extended and the others flexed**.
  The pain disappears when all fingers pull in the same (extended) position. This is the "lumbrical stress/shift test".
- **Outcome:** in 60 patients (57 climbers), grades were I (microtrauma) 30%, II (fibre disruption) 53% and III
  (musculotendinous disruption) 17%. All recovered fully with adapted functional therapy, and grade III took significantly
  longer (Lutter et al. 2018) **[CS, LoE IV]**. Healing times in weeks were not available in the accessible text.
- **Treatment:** symptomatic care, taping, and gentle stretching started early, "done in the same way that the injury was
  provoked, but with much less load" (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Review]**.

### 1.5 PIP joint capsulitis/synovitis, collateral-ligament and volar-plate injuries
- **Capsulitis/synovitis** is among the top three finger diagnoses in climbers (6.1% of injuries in Schöffl et al. 2003; reported
  as 6–10% in Vagy 2023).
  - **Presentation:** a dull ache, stiffness and swelling of the PIP joint (sometimes the DIP in full crimp). The pain is often
    dorsal or lateral, eases with warm-up, and is provoked by end-range flexion/extension overpressure. Onset is gradual with
    crimp volume, or acute after a jam or knuckle strike.
  - **Treatment and course:** one case report describes 2 weeks of unloading, mobility work (traction/oscillation), progressive
    strength, reduced crimping, and unrestricted climbing at 6 weeks (Vagy 2023) **[CS, n=1]**.
  - **Long-term:** chronic capsulitis may precede osteoarthritis (Vagy 2023). Long-term climbers show adaptive changes and
    osteophytes, with dorsal osteophytes irritating the extensor hood ("extensor hood syndrome": dorsal PIP/DIP pain). This is
    usually treated conservatively and resolves in about 3–5 weeks (Schweizer 2012; Schöffl et al. 2020) **[CS/Review]**.
- **Collateral-ligament injury:** caused by a sideways or twisting load (pocket torsion, a fall onto a finger).
  - **Exam:** tenderness over the side of the joint; valgus/varus stress at 30° PIP flexion (with the MCP at 90°) compared with
    the opposite finger.
  - **Treatment:** stable joint → **buddy-tape 2–4 weeks**; **unstable joint or a child → refer** (Leggit & Meko 2006; Mergoum et al.
    2025) **[Review]**.
  - **Combined injuries:** a collateral-ligament injury together with a pulley rupture is classed as Schöffl grade IV.
- **Volar plate** (hyperextension or dorsal dislocation): stable → extension-block splint from 30° flexion, extended about 10° per
  week for 2–4 weeks, then buddy taping. Unstable or large fragment → refer (Leggit & Meko 2006; Mergoum et al. 2025) **[Review]**.

### 1.6 FDP avulsion ("jersey finger") and flexor-tendon strains ("flexor unit" strains)
- **FDP avulsion.** In climbers it mostly affects the **ring finger**, typically when a finger catches in a pocket or a
  degenerated tendon fails (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Review]**.
  - **Test:** hold the PIP straight and ask the patient to flex the fingertip (DIP). If the DIP does not move, the FDP is
    disrupted (Leggit & Meko 2006).
  - **Management:** every suspected case needs **hand-surgery referral**, because prognosis worsens with delay. Reviews advise early
    surgery, within about **7–10 days** (Mergoum et al. 2025, for retracted/zone-II FDP injuries; Leggit & Meko 2006).
    Avulsion fractures always need surgery (Mergoum et al. 2025) **[Review]**.
- **Flexor-tendon/muscle strain.** This is a peer-reviewed description; "flexor unit strain" is a practitioner term **[EO]** that
  I could not find defined in the peer-reviewed literature.
  - **Mechanism:** usually a sudden load in the **hanging/sloper (open) position**, such as a foot slip.
  - **Signs:** pain runs along the course of the flexor tendon into the palm or forearm. It is **worse in the hanging position
    and sometimes absent in crimp**, the opposite of the pulley pattern.
  - **Course:** recovery can be prolonged and recurrence is high. Treatment is conservative.
  - **Complications:** partial tears can form nodules and cause triggering. Complete tears are rare and need surgery
    (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Review]**.
  - A cadaver model confirms that asymmetric postures can load lumbricals and FDP together (Carnicero et al. 2024) **[Lab]**.

### 1.7 Epiphyseal (growth-plate) stress fractures in adolescents
- This is the **most common sport-specific injury in adolescent climbers** (Schöffl et al. 2022; Schöffl et al. 2024).
  - Of 20 injured climbers under 15, 14 had an epiphyseal fracture (mean age 14, range 12–15) (Schöffl et al. 2015) **[CS]**.
  - A scoping review (11 studies, 149 injuries in 98 climbers) found a mean age of 14 (10–18). **98% were at the PIP joint**, and
    the middle finger (64%) and ring finger (28%) were most affected. 96% had an insidious onset and 74% were fractures
    (Jaini et al. 2026) **[SR-scoping]**.
- **Presentation:** **no trauma**; pain, sometimes swelling, at the PIP joint (dorsal base of the middle phalanx). The fracture is
  typically Salter-Harris III (Hochholzer & Schöffl 2005: 24 cases, age 14.5 ± 0.9, 23 boys) **[CS]**.
- **Risk factors:** intensive power training at this age (campus board, frequent crimping), and regular IFSC speed-wall training,
  which was associated with growth-plate injury (p = 0.02) (Meyers et al. 2020, WEM) **[Cross-sect]**.
  - Growth plates close at about 17–19 years (Schweizer 2012).
  - Only 15% of youth competitors correctly named growth-plate injury as the common youth injury, and 6% knew the safe age for
    double-dyno campusing (Meyers et al. 2020, IJERPH) **[survey]**.
- **Course:**
  - Bärtschi et al. 2019 (28 climbers): mean radiological union **35 weeks**, sprains about 24 weeks. Load reduction lasted
    3–12 months, and 8 climbers had residual articular incongruency **[CS]**.
  - Schöffl et al. 2022 (37 injuries): algorithm-guided treatment (28 non-operative, 9 surgical) gave union in all cases.
    Return to sport came 40 ± 65 days after the start of treatment **[CS, LoE 4]**.
  - Across studies, return took 2 weeks to 6 months, and 91% returned (Jaini et al. 2026).
  - A return-to-climb protocol based on expert opinion exists (Meyers, Schöffl et al. 2020, CSMR; only the abstract was accessible).
- **Prevention:** "eliminate intensive power training" in this age group and avoid full crimp. An adolescent with PIP pain and
  no trauma should avoid crimping until the pain resolves or the plates close, and needs imaging (Hochholzer & Schöffl 2005;
  Schweizer 2012) **[CS/Review]**.

### 1.8 Other differentials an app must not miss

| Condition | Clue | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Bone marrow oedema / stress reaction (hand, wrist) | diffuse hand/wrist pain in high-level climbers. MRI needed; resolves in 3–4 months with load reduction | Lutter et al. 2018 **[CS]** |
| Hamate hook fracture | ulnar palm/wrist pain during or after climbing | Lutter et al. 2016 **[CS]** |
| Flexor-sheath ganglion, trigger finger/nodule | lump, catching or locking | Schöffl & Schöffl 2007; Schöffl et al. 2020 **[Review]** |
| Osteoarthritis / osteophytes | older long-term climbers, dorsal joint pain | Schöffl et al. 2004 **[Cross-sect]**; Schöffl et al. 2020 |
| Infectious flexor tenosynovitis | after a cut or puncture: fusiform swelling, finger held flexed, pain on passive extension (Kanavel signs). **Emergency** | standard clinical teaching |
| Fracture / dislocation | direct trauma, deformity, bony tenderness → X-ray | Crowley 2012; Bouredoucen & Schöffl 2026 **[Review]** |

More than 20 diagnoses need to be considered in finger pain in climbers (Schöffl & Schöffl 2007) **[Review]**.

**Quick differential matrix (for triage logic):**

| Pattern | Most likely | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| Sudden pain on a crimp/foot slip, palm side of proximal or middle phalanx, crimp hurts more than open hand | Pulley (A2/A4) | Provoked by crimp; tenderness over the pulley |
| Same, plus a pop, swelling/bruising, marked weakness | Pulley rupture (grade II–III) | Needs ultrasound |
| Visible bowstringing, loss of flexion | Multiple pulleys (grade IV) | Urgent hand surgeon |
| Pain along the whole finger into the palm or forearm, gradual onset, sheath swelling | Tenosynovitis | US halo; pain with volume rather than one grip |
| Palm pain only with mixed finger positions (pockets) | Lumbrical | Positive lumbrical shift test; pain gone with all fingers extended |
| Pain worse in open/hanging grips after a foot slip, along the tendon | Flexor tendon/muscle strain | Opposite pattern to pulley |
| Cannot bend the fingertip | FDP avulsion | Urgent surgical referral |
| Swollen, stiff PIP, pain at end-range, gradual onset | PIP capsulitis/synovitis | Joint, not sheath; dorsal/lateral |
| Side-of-joint pain after a twist, laxity | Collateral ligament | Stress test; instability → refer |
| Age < 18, PIP pain, no trauma | **Epiphyseal stress fracture until proven otherwise** | X-ray/MRI before any loading |

## 2. Diagnostics

### 2.1 What clinicians test
1. **History:** mechanism (crimp, foot slip, pocket), pop, time course, previous injury, training load.
2. **Inspection:** swelling, bruising, deformity, visible bowstringing.
3. **Palpation:** point tenderness over A2/A3/A4, the joint lines and the lumbricals. Grading by the pressure needed is
   used in the Cooper schema.
4. **ROM:** active and passive, full fist and full extension.
5. **Resisted tests:**
   - FDS and FDP tested separately (FDP: DIP flexion with the PIP held).
   - Resisted flexion in open, half-crimp and full-crimp positions.
   - Lumbrical shift test.
   - Collateral stress at 30° flexion.
6. **Grip strength vs the other side:** a 2024 review suggests combining clinical signs, ultrasound and grip strength. Its abstract
   reports that "a grip deficit of 41.4% corresponds to a pulley injury", a figure I could not verify in full text (Bellomo et
   al. 2024) **[SR, 14 heterogeneous studies]**.

### 2.2 Imaging: when and what
- **Ultrasound is the first-line imaging for suspected pulley injury**, with MRI if ultrasound is inconclusive (Miro et al.
  2021; Schöffl et al. 2020; Bellomo et al. 2024) **[Review/SR]**. The examination is dynamic, in **forced flexion**, measuring the
  tendon–bone distance (TBD).
  - Dynamic US in 64 extreme climbers detected 16/16 complete A2 ruptures, 9/9 complete A4, 6/7 combined A2+A3 and 15/15
    incomplete A2. **Sensitivity 98%, specificity 100%** (threshold > 1.0 mm; Klauser et al. 2002) **[Cohort, diagnostic]**.
  - In a cadaver study, A2 and A4 injuries were detected with sensitivity 90%/94% and specificity 100%/97%. The **general rupture
    criterion is TBD > 2 mm in forced flexion** (Schöffl I et al. 2018, as reported in Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Lab]**.
  - Typical pre-treatment TBD in complete ruptures: A2 4.4 ± 1.0 mm, A4 2.9 ± 0.7 mm (Schneeberger & Schweizer 2016) **[CS]**.
- **Bamberg algorithm** (Schöffl et al. 2020, Fig. 5) **[Review]**:
  1. Suspected pulley rupture → **X-ray** (exclude fracture).
  2. **Ultrasound**, with MRI if questionable.
  3. TBD < 2 mm → strain → symptomatic therapy. TBD > 2 mm with a single rupture → conservative therapy. Multiple ruptures →
     surgical repair.
- **X-ray first** for fractures, avulsions, osteophytes and **all adolescents with joint pain**. Use two orthogonal views plus an
  oblique, and MRI if an occult fracture is suspected (Jaini et al. 2026; Bouredoucen & Schöffl 2026) **[SR-scoping/Review]**.
  3-T MRI is valuable for epiphyseal injuries and their follow-up (Bayer et al. 2013) **[CS]**.
- **MRI** is for diffuse hand/wrist pain (bone marrow oedema), inconclusive US, and complex injuries (Lutter et al. 2018; Miro et al. 2021).
- **Timing matters.** For multiple ruptures, conservative ring therapy "must be started **within a few days** after the
  trauma and be performed strictly for 6 to 8 weeks". Otherwise surgery is the standard (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[Review]**.
  FDP avulsions need surgery within days. An early diagnosis therefore changes management; it is not just reassurance.

### 2.3 Red flags: prompt medical assessment needed

| Red flag | Why | Urgency (synthesis) |
|---|---|---|
| Cannot actively flex the DIP (fingertip) | FDP avulsion | **Same/next day** hand surgeon (repair window ~7–10 days) |
| Visible bowstringing, or flexion contracture / loss of active flexion | multiple-pulley rupture (grade IV) | **Within days** (ring within a few days, or surgery) |
| Audible pop **plus** swelling/bruising or marked weakness | grade II–IV rupture | Ultrasound **within about a week** (expert synthesis; the grade decides ring vs tape vs surgery) |
| Gross swelling, deformity, crepitus, bony point tenderness, dislocation | fracture / dislocation / volar-plate avulsion | **Same day** X-ray |
| Joint unstable sideways | collateral-ligament rupture | Prompt referral |
| Numbness, tingling, pale/blue/cold finger (also under tape or ring) | digital nerve/vessel injury or compression | **Same day** (remove tape/ring first) |
| Wound or puncture with spreading redness, warmth, fever, finger held bent, pain on passive extension | infectious tenosynovitis | **Emergency** |
| **Age < 18 (still growing) with finger-joint pain**, especially PIP and no trauma | epiphyseal stress fracture | X-ray (± MRI) **before any loading program** |
| Severe pain at rest or at night, or pain > 5/10 in daily life | severe injury or non-mechanical cause | Prompt assessment |
| Locking/catching, or a lump | trigger nodule, partial tendon tear, ganglion | Routine–prompt |
| Several fingers or joints, morning stiffness, no climbing explanation | inflammatory arthritis etc. | Routine–prompt |
| Not improving after 2–3 weeks of sensible unloading, or getting worse at any time | wrong diagnosis or higher grade | Assessment / ultrasound |

## 3. Treatment per grade and type

### 3.1 Pulley injuries: synthesised timeline table
Exact values differ slightly between reproductions of the Schöffl table, so the ranges are shown with their sources.
**All values are population guide-lines from case series, not individual clearances.**

| Grade | Immobilisation | Protection | Functional therapy | Light/easy climbing | Full climbing | Tape after return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** strain | none (Bosco 2022) | tape | 2–4 wk | ~2–4 wk (Schöffl, via secondary reproductions) to 2–6 wk (Mergoum 2025) | **~6 wk** (Schöffl; Mergoum 2025; Crowley 2012) | ~3 months |
| **II** partial A2/A3, complete A4 | **10 days** (Bosco 2022); 10–14 d (Mergoum 2025) | tape | cautious 2–4 wk | after the functional phase (Schöffl) to 6–8 wk (Mergoum) | **6–8 wk** (Larsson 2022, summarising Schöffl) to 8–10 wk (Mergoum) | ~3 months |
| **III** complete A2/A3 | **10–14 days** (Crowley 2012; Bosco 2022) | **thermoplastic or soft-cast ring** (Bosco; Crowley) | up to 4 wk | **6–8 wk** (Crowley) to 8–12 wk (Mergoum) | **~3 months** (all sources) | **~6 months** (Larsson; Crowley) |
| **IV** multiple, or with lumbrical/ligament | post-op 2 wk | ring | 4 wk | ~4 months (Hooper A2 manual, EO, and secondary summaries of Schöffl) | **~6 months** post-op (Mergoum 2025) | **≥ 12 months** (Mergoum 2025) |

Supporting facts:
- **Healing time of a pulley is 2–3 months; full load-bearing is expected after 4–6 months** (Schöffl et al. 2020; Schweizer 2012) **[Review]**.
  This is the main biological reason not to compress return-to-limit into 6 weeks for grade II–III.
- Schöffl's cohort: 90–91% of grade 1–3 patients were back in sport at 3 months with little or no pain. All grade-4 patients had
  surgery (Larsson et al. 2022, summarising Schöffl 2003/2004; GRADE certainty very low) **[CS]**.
- **Pulley-protection splint (ring):**
  - In 47 complete ruptures, the splint reduced TBD from 4.4 to 2.3 mm (A2) and from 2.9 to 2.1 mm (A4). Finger ROM and
    strength did not differ from the contralateral side.
  - **38/43 regained their previous climbing level, on average after 8.8 months** (Schneeberger & Schweizer 2016) **[CS]**.
  - The standard regimen is a ring for about 2 months, then pulley tape, with bowstringing reduced by about 50% (A2) and 40% (A4)
    (Schöffl et al. 2020; Schweizer 2012).
- **Climbing while wearing a ring orthosis** (grade I–III A2, 12 weeks, ≥3 sessions/week) let 4/4 participants improve without
  interrupting climbing (Hartnett et al. 2024) **[CS, n=4]**. This is interesting but far too small to generalise.
- **Surgery:**
  - Indications: grade IV and multiple ruptures (otherwise flexion contracture), and some grade III injuries with clinical
    bowstringing or slow healing (Schöffl et al. 2003; Mergoum et al. 2025) **[CS/Review]**.
  - Graft techniques: Widström "loop and a half", Weilby. Rehabilitation takes several months (Schöffl et al. 2003, 2020).
  - Graft encircling techniques can cause bone resorption or osteitis of the proximal phalanx (Cerasani et al. 2024 **[CS]**;
    Lutter & Schöffl 2015).
  - Direct suture in 8 elite climbers allowed return to pre-injury level after a mean 5.8 months (3–8) (Balandier et al. 2026) **[CS]**.
  - Some climbers with multiple ruptures returned to their previous level conservatively, if there was no clinical bowstringing
    and ring therapy started immediately (Schöffl et al. 2020) **[CS/EO]**.

### 3.2 Taping (H-tape, circular): how much does it protect?
- **Systematic review** (9 studies, 206 climbers plus cadavers): **low-to-moderate certainty** that taping **reduces bowstringing by
  roughly 15–22%**. Certainty is **very low** for pain or return-to-sport benefit, and **no study tested taping for injury prevention**
  (Larsson et al. 2022) **[SR]**.
- **H-tape after rupture:**
  - TBD 3.77 → 3.19 mm (**−16%**); circular and figure-8 tape had no significant effect.
  - **+13% crimp force** in injured fingers, with no change in the hanging/open position (Schöffl I et al. 2007) **[small crossover, n=8/12]**.
- **Circular tape:**
  - Over A2: bowstringing −2.8% (not significant), absorbing ~11% of the bowstringing force.
  - At the distal end of the proximal phalanx: bowstringing −22%, absorbing ~12% of force.
  - Schweizer called it "minimally effective… probably ineffective in preventing pulley ruptures" (Schweizer 2000) **[Lab, 16 fingers]**.
- **Cadaver failure tests:** circular tape did not change pulley rupture force (Warme & Brooks 2000) **[Lab]**. H-tape was "not effective as
  prophylaxis… or as a stabilizing treatment" for 50% distal A2 tears (Salas et al. 2023) **[Lab, 14 hand pairs]**.
- **Prevention in healthy fingers:** tape is "very unlikely to be effective", one source even suggesting increased risk. Its main
  benefit is limiting PIP flexion to about 80–90° when applied near or over the PIP (Schöffl et al. 2020; Schweizer 2012) **[Review]**.
- **Practical rule (Schöffl):** after a pulley injury, tape at the distal end of the injured pulley or as an H-tape at the PIP, during
  climbing, for about 3 months (I–II), ~6 months (III) and ≥12 months (IV).
- **App wording:** "tape modestly reduces tendon lift-off and may make crimping feel stronger. It does **not** make an injured
  pulley safe to overload."

| Injury | Conservative core | Escalation | Source |

## 4. Rehab loading

### 4.1 What the evidence does and does not say
- The leading clinical commentary (Ehiogu, Schöffl & Jones 2023; abstract only) states plainly that "there is a **paucity of
  guidance** clearly describing the rehabilitation and physical preparation parameters to return to sport". It argues that management should rest on
  progressive loading and strength-and-conditioning principles **[Review]**.
- **No RCT, cohort or case series has compared loading protocols for pulley injuries.** Every parameter below comes from:
  - (a) tendon/ligament science in other tissues;
  - (b) training studies in healthy climbers;
  - (c) practitioner protocols **[EO]**.
- **Tendon and ligament biology:**
  - Healthy tendons adapt in stiffness mainly according to **load magnitude, not contraction type**, and adaptation takes ≥ 12 weeks
    (Bohm et al. 2015 **[SR/MA]**).
  - Loading above 70% of 1RM gave large stiffness/modulus gains; low intensity gave moderate stiffness gains (Lazarczuk et al. 2022 **[SR/MA]**, healthy lower-limb tendons).
  - Healing ligaments are restored by **scar tissue that is biologically and mechanically inferior**, which remodels over months
    (Frank 2004) **[Review]**.
- **Isometrics** are not superior to isotonic exercise for tendinopathy (Clifford et al. 2020 **[SR/MA]**). The isometric
  analgesia finding (Rio et al. 2015: 5 × 45 s at 70% MVC, n=6 **[RCT crossover]**) has not translated into consistent clinical
  superiority. Isometric pick-up loading is used because it is **controllable and measurable**, not because it heals faster.

### 4.2 Devices and grips
- **Pick-up / no-hang devices** (lifting a block or loading pin, or a force gauge) allow **one-arm loading with the feet on the
  floor, kilogram-precise increments and immediate release**. Hangboards need weight removal (pulley/feet) to get loads that low.
  No study compares these devices for rehab **[EO]**.
- **Measurement reliability:** maximal isometric finger strength tests on fixed-depth **~20–23 mm edges** are highly reliable
  (ICC median range 0.85–0.99; Pérez-Cordero et al. 2025 **[SR]**). This supports "always the same ~20 mm edge" for monitoring.
- **Grip progression:** open hand / 3-finger drag → half crimp → full crimp. This follows the biomechanical load ranking
  (Vigouroux et al. 2006; Schweizer 2001) **[Lab]** and is used by all practitioner protocols **[EO]**.
  - **A4 injuries:** open hand still loads A4 (only 4× less than crimp), so open hand is not automatically pain-free.
  - **Lumbrical injuries:** the problem grips are *mixed* finger positions (pockets, two-finger), not crimps.
  - **Flexor-strain injuries:** open/hanging grips may be the provocative ones.
- **Smooth loading:** ramp up gradually, never jerk, and lower in a controlled way. This is consistent with the injury mechanism
  (eccentric/shock loads) **[Review/EO]**.

### 4.4 Intensity, volume, frequency: what is defensible
- **Strength-phase dose.** 6 × 10 s at 80–95% with 2 min rest is the standard "max-hang" dose. It improves finger strength in
  *healthy* climbers:
  - Gilmore 2024: +3.2% in 4–16 weeks **[Cohort, retrospective, n=526]**.
  - Mundry 2021: adding **1.25 kg per week** beat climbing alone **[RCT, n=30]**.
  - López-Rivera & González-Badillo 2019 **[RCT]**; Langer et al. 2023 **[SR]**.
  - For injured fingers this dose is a *late-phase target*, not a starting dose.
- **Frequency: "multiple short sessions/day, ≥ 6 h apart".**
  - The rationale is in vitro: in engineered ligaments, signalling peaked after 10 min of loading and the cells stayed **refractory
    for up to 6 h**. Ten minutes every 6 h increased collagen more than continuous loading (Paxton et al. 2012 **[Lab]**).
    Baar extrapolates this to "short (< 10 min) activity with ~6 h rest" (Baar 2017 **[Review]**).
  - Counterpoint: in humans, tendon collagen synthesis after one exercise bout **peaks at 24 h and is still elevated at 72 h**
    (Miller et al. 2005 **[Lab, human]**). Twice-daily sessions are therefore *plausible but unproven* to beat daily sessions for
    pulley healing.
  - Treat ≥ 6 h spacing as a sensible scheduling rule (it also caps total load), not as a proven healing accelerator **[EO]**.
- **Healthy-hand training.** Training the uninjured hand is worthwhile: unilateral upper-limb training gives a small strength
  gain in the untrained limb (cross-education; SMD ≈ 0.42; Hernando-Garijo et al. 2026 **[SR/MA]**). It also keeps the reference
  hand from detraining.

### 4.5 Pain monitoring: pulley vs tendinopathy vs others

| Condition | During loading | After / next morning | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tendinopathy** (Achilles model) | pain allowed **up to 5/10** | after-session pain ≤ 5/10 must **subside by next morning**; pain/stiffness must not increase week to week | **RCT** (Silbernagel et al. 2007). Continued pain-monitored loading had no negative effect |
| **Pulley strain/rupture** | **0–2/10** (Hooper ≤ 2/10; Climbing Doctor 0/10 early, later < 3/10 lasting ≤ 3 min); no sharp pain | no increase after the session or **next morning** | **[EO] only.** No study tests thresholds for pulleys. Lower than tendinopathy because this is an acutely healing ligament with rupture risk |
| **Tenosynovitis** | keep low; avoid volumes that provoke | symptoms should settle; load reduction is the main therapy | **[Cohort]** for load reduction (Mohn 2022); no threshold data |
| **Lumbrical** | stretch/load in the provoking position "with much less load" | not worse next day | **[Review/EO]** |
| **PIP capsulitis** | comfort-guided; avoid end-range crimp | 24-h pain tracked | **[CS]** (Vagy 2023) |
| **Adolescent joint pain** | **no self-guided loading**: medical pathway, pain-free only after clearance | n/a | **[CS/Review]** |

**Monitoring tenderness.** Cooper & LaStayo grade palpation pain by the pressure needed. Pressing with the opposite thumb until
its nail blanches is a reproducible "maximal pressure" that an app can describe for daily self-tests of pressure pain at the same
spot **[EO; reliability not studied]**.

### 4.6 Finding the starting load (all **[EO]**, combine them)
1. **Pain-limited ramp:** start very light in open hand (e.g. 2–5 kg) and increase in small steps (1–2 kg) until either the effort
   is ~3–4/10 or pain reaches 1–2/10. Then go one step back. That is the working load.
2. **Relative to a reference:** early tissue loading ~20–40% of the reference max (Abrahangs ≈ 40% in healthy fingers).
   Strength phase builds toward ≥ 70–90% (tendon-adaptation literature: Lazarczuk 2022). Here the reference is the pre-injury
   max on the same edge if logged, otherwise the uninjured hand.
3. **Effort cap:** no fatigue at the start of each rep (Hooper). Measured as RPE, e.g. ≤ 5–6/10 for 10 s, and only if pain stays ≤ 2/10.
4. **Test condition:** keep it constant (same edge, grip, tape or no tape). Tape raised crimp force by 13% in injured fingers
   (Schöffl 2007), so mixing taped and untaped tests distorts progress.

### 4.7 Comparing to the healthy hand
- In 36 male climbers, the strongest hand was on average **4.1% stronger**, with handedness explaining 89% of the difference
  (Hartley et al. 2023) **[Cross-sect]**.
- The classic "10% rule" (dominant hand ~10% stronger) holds only for right-handers (12.7%). Left-handers average ~0%
  (Petersen et al. 1989) **[Cross-sect]**.
- **The uninjured side can overestimate recovery.** After ACL injury, 57% of patients met 90% limb symmetry at 6 months, but only
  29% met 90% of *estimated pre-injury* capacity (Wellsandt et al. 2017) **[Cohort]**.
- **App rule:** compare with **pre-injury data** first. If only the healthy hand is available, use it with a handedness correction:
  if the injured hand is the dominant one, the target is ≥ ~100% of the healthy (non-dominant) hand **[EO]**.

### 4.8 Progression rules and reintroduction milestones
- **Week-to-week progression [EO]:** progress only if the pain criteria were met in all sessions and the next morning was not
  worse. Repeat the week if borderline; drop back one step after a flare. This mirrors the RCT-tested pain-monitoring logic
  (Silbernagel 2007), adapted to lower thresholds. Typical step: +1–2.5 kg or about 5–10% per week (Mundry 2021 used +1.25 kg/week in healthy climbers).
- **Time floors by grade.** Do not let load criteria alone accelerate past the biological windows in §3.1, especially for
  grade II–IV (pulley healing 2–3 months, full load 4–6 months).
- **Reintroduction order [EO, consistent across Schöffl/Hooper/Climbing Doctor]:**
  1. Easy, open-hand climbing on big holds, ≤ vertical, 2+ grades below onsight, taped.
  2. Half crimp on moderate terrain, 1 grade below onsight.
  3. Crimps on overhangs up to onsight.
  4. **Board, limit moves, dynamic moves to crimps.**
  5. **Campus** and **full crimp** last. Full crimp is optional; Schweizer 2012 advises avoiding pronounced full crimp
     (PIP ≤ 80–90°, DIP 5–15°) even when healthy.
- **Return to performance takes longer than return to climbing.** After complete ruptures treated with a splint, the mean time to
  regain the previous level was **8.8 months** (Schneeberger & Schweizer 2016).

### 4.9 Nutrition adjunct: gelatin/collagen + vitamin C
- **15 g vitamin-C-enriched gelatin** (≈ 48 mg vitamin C) taken **1 h before 6 min of rope skipping** roughly doubled the rise of
  the systemic collagen marker PINP: +153% vs +54% with placebo at 4 h (Shaw et al. 2017) **[RCT crossover, n=8 men]**.
  - Limitations: PINP is mainly a bone marker, so the effect is not tendon-specific. There were no injury outcomes, and serum
    amino acids peak about 1 h after 15 g.
- A replication with 15 g gelatin or hydrolysed collagen showed a ~20% PINP rise that was **not significant** (Lis & Baar 2019 **[RCT, n=10]**).
- Collagen did **not** increase muscle connective-tissue protein synthesis:
  - 30 g after exercise: Aussieker et al. 2023 **[RCT, n=45]**.
  - 2 × 15 g/day over 1 week of training: Kirmse et al. 2024 **[RCT, n=25]**.
  - Holwerda & van Loon 2022 **[Review]**.
- **Structural tendon outcomes:** a 2026 systematic review (8 RCTs, almost all men) found greater tendon CSA and stiffness with
  15–30 g collagen + ≥ 50 mg vitamin C combined with **high-intensity** resistance training over 3–15 weeks, and no effect on
  strength (Buchalski et al. 2026 **[SR]**).
- **For tears/injuries the evidence is "sparse, heterogeneous, and low certainty"** (Latini et al. 2026 **[SR]**). There are no
  finger or pulley data.
- **Verdict:** low-risk, optional, possibly helpful, **unproven for pulley healing**. If offered, take it ~60 min before the loading
  session, as studied. It must never be framed as a reason to load harder or return sooner.

## 5. Return-to-climbing and return-to-performance criteria
- **No validated return-to-climb criteria exist for any finger injury.** An international e-Delphi to define rehab-phase,
  return-to-sport and return-to-performance criteria for upper-limb injuries in climbers has a published protocol (Ehiogu,
  Schöffl et al. 2025) **[Consensus protocol, results pending]**. The authors describe the climbing-specific literature as
  "very sparse".
- **Criteria used in practice or plausible** (synthesis; evidence noted):

| Domain | Typical criterion | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Time since injury | at least the grade-specific minimum in §3.1 (I ~6 wk, II ~6–10 wk, III ~3 months, IV ~6 months) for full climbing | **[CS]** clinic series (Schöffl; Mergoum) |
| Pain | no/minimal pain in daily life; ≤ 1–2/10 during climbing-specific loading, with no next-day flare | **[EO]** |
| Tenderness/swelling | none or minimal with firm (nail-blanching) pressure; no swelling | **[EO]** (Cooper schema) |
| ROM | full, pain-free fist and extension; no flexion contracture | **[Review]** (Mergoum 2025: "improvement in ROM is crucial before return to play") |
| Strength | injured ≥ ~90–95% of pre-injury or healthy hand (handedness-corrected) on the same edge/grip, in the grip being returned to | **[EO]**, borrowed from ACL: passing all RTS tests > 90% was associated with 5.6% vs 38.2% reinjury, HR 0.16, p = 0.075 (Grindem et al. 2016 **[Cohort]**). Hooper: match pre-injury metrics |
| Imaging (ruptures) | reduced TBD after splinting (Schneeberger 2016) | **[CS]**. Routine re-imaging is not standard |
| Functional | progressive on-wall exposure (easy → moderate → hard → board/limit) completed without flare | **[EO]** |
| Protection | continue tape/ring for 3 / 6 / ≥ 12 months (grades I–II / III / IV) | **[CS/Review]** |

- **Return to performance** (previous limit grade) often takes months longer: mean 8.8 months after complete ruptures
  (Schneeberger 2016), and 5.8 months after direct pulley suture (Balandier 2026).

## 7. Triage questions for the app

Purpose: to estimate the likely injury type and severity, decide whether self-guided loading is appropriate at all, and choose a
safe starting phase. **This is decision support, not diagnosis.** If a clinician has already diagnosed or graded the injury, the
clinician's grade overrides the app's estimate.

### 7.2 Answers that must trigger "please see a doctor/physio first"

**Emergency or same day (no loading program):**
- Infection signs after a wound (Q16).
- Numbness or circulation change (Q15).
- Deformity, dislocation, unstable joint, or suspected fracture (Q3 blow/fall + Q9 marked, Q10 deformed/unstable).
- Cannot flex the fingertip (Q7 = no). Hand surgeon; repair is time-critical (~7–10 days).

**Within days (pause self-guided loading until seen):**
- Visible bowstringing or loss of active flexion (Q8 = yes or unsure with Q10 limited). Possible multiple-pulley rupture; the
  ring must start within days, or surgery.
- Pop plus moderate/marked swelling or bruising, or "severe" on Q11/Q12/Q14. Suspected grade II–III rupture; ultrasound
  within ~1 week. In the meantime the app may offer only protection advice (rest from climbing, pain-free ROM, buddy/H-tape),
  **no loading progression**.
- Age < 18 / still growing with any finger-joint pain (Q1 + Q6 joint). X-ray (± MRI); epiphyseal stress fracture.
- Heel-of-palm or wrist pain (Q6). Hamate fracture or bone-marrow oedema; X-ray/MRI.

**Routine:**
- No improvement after 2–3 weeks, or worse at any time (Q17/Q18).
- Locking, catching or a lump.
- Several fingers or joints without a clear climbing cause.
- Recurrent injury of the same structure.
- Lumbrical or tenosynovitis pattern not settling.

### 7.3 Estimating a safe starting phase (synthesis, [EO])

| Triage result | Likely category | App entry phase |
|---|---|---|
| No red flags, mild pattern (daily pain 0–2, crimp ≤ 2/10, pain only on very hard pressure, full ROM, no pop) | Grade I-like strain / overuse | Off-wall gentle loading from day 1–3; easy taped open-hand climbing after ~1–2 weeks if pain criteria are met; full-climbing floor ~6 wk |
| No red flags, moderate pattern (daily 3–5, half crimp 2–5, moderate tenderness, ≤ 25% ROM loss) or pop without major swelling | Possible grade II | **Recommend ultrasound.** Protection ~10–14 d (tape, pain-free ROM, open-hand loading only if 0–1/10); climbing floor ~4–6 wk easy, full ~8–10 wk |

## 8. What an app can responsibly recommend
- **Triage first, program second.** Screen red flags (§2.3, §7.2) and route to a clinician *before* offering any loading. Make
  "see a doctor/physio" a real outcome, not a footnote.
- **Use grade- or severity-specific pathways with time floors.** Draw them from the clinic series: full climbing ~6 wk (I), ~6–10 wk (II),
  ~3 months (III), ~6 months (IV, post-op). Healing takes 2–3 months and full load 4–6 months. Progress on criteria, but never faster than the floor.
- **Recommend ultrasound for suspected ruptures** (pop, swelling/bruising, marked weakness). It is the first-line, highly accurate
  test, and the grade changes management.
- **Offer progressive, pain-monitored isometric loading on a pick-up / no-hang device:**
  - same ~20 mm edge, smooth loading;
  - open hand → half crimp → full crimp (last, optional);
  - early holds of 10–30 s at low effort, later 6 × 10 s strength sets, 2–3 sessions/week;
  - small weekly increments (~1–2.5 kg or 5–10%).
  Label the specific numbers as practitioner-based starting points.
- **Pain rules for pulleys:** ≤ 2/10 during (0–1/10 early after a rupture), no sharp pain, not worse next morning, tenderness not
  rising. Progress only when all criteria hold; repeat the week or step back otherwise. Use different, documented rules per
  injury type (tendinopathy-style thresholds do **not** transfer to acute pulley tears).
- **A daily log** of standardised pressure pain (same spot, thumbnail-blanching pressure), session load, pain, and the
  next-morning value. Trends should drive progression and "see a clinician" prompts (not better at 2–3 weeks, or worse).
- **References for loads:** prefer **pre-injury strength data** (CruxCoach may already have it). Otherwise test the healthy hand early and
  retest later, with a handedness correction (~4% typical asymmetry). Don't max-test the injured hand early.
- **Graded on-wall re-exposure:** easy open-hand terrain (≤ vertical, 2+ grades below onsight, taped) → half crimp on moderate terrain →
  crimps on overhangs → board/limit → campus and full crimp last.
- **Taping:** H-tape (at the PIP) or tape at the distal end of the injured pulley **whenever climbing** after a pulley injury, for about 3 / 6 / ≥ 12
  months (grades I–II / III / IV). Described honestly as a modest reduction of tendon lift-off (~15–22%), not a shield.
- **Warm-up:** a graded climbing warm-up (3–4 routes or 8–12 boulders of increasing difficulty) before crimp-intensive sessions, and
  avoiding habitual full crimp (PIP ≤ 80–90°).
- **Healthy-hand training** during rehab (small cross-education benefit; keeps the reference honest).
- **Optional gelatin/collagen (~15 g) with ~50 mg vitamin C ~60 min before loading:** low-risk, unproven for finger injuries,
  never tied to faster progression.
- **For non-pulley diagnoses, only general, conservative guidance:**
  - lumbrical: avoid mixed-finger grips, gentle stretch;
  - tenosynovitis: load reduction;
  - capsulitis: unload, ROM;
  - stable collateral sprain: buddy-tape 2–4 weeks.
  Prompt a clinician visit if not settling in 2–3 weeks.
- **Youth:** education only (growth-plate risk, no campus or heavy crimp training during the growth spurt, **report any finger-joint
  pain to a doctor**). No self-guided rehab program for under-18s with joint pain.

## 9. What it must not claim
- That it can **diagnose** a pulley rupture or **assign a Schöffl grade**. Grading needs a clinician and ultrasound/MRI; clinical
  severity scores are unvalidated against imaging.
- That **no bowstringing means no rupture**, or that **little pain means a minor injury**. Single ruptures show no visible
  bowstringing, and full tears can be fairly painless.
- That its loading parameters (sets, reps, %, frequency, ≥ 6 h spacing, 30-s "tendon" holds) are **evidence-based for finger
  injuries**. They are extrapolated and expert-derived; no trial has tested them.
- That **pain ≤ 2/10 is proven safe** for pulley loading, or that pain-free means healed.
- That **isometrics heal faster** or reliably relieve pain.
- That **taping prevents (re-)injury** or makes crimping safe. Evidence shows only modest bowstringing reduction; cadaver studies
  show no protection against rupture; prophylactic taping of healthy fingers is not supported.
- That **gelatin/collagen + vitamin C speeds pulley or tendon healing**.
- That a **timeline is guaranteed** ("back to limit in 6 weeks"). Timelines are population ranges, and return to the previous level
  after complete ruptures averaged ~9 months.
- That reaching **90% of the healthy hand** (or any single test) is a **validated clearance criterion**. No validated
  return-to-climb criteria exist; a Delphi is still in progress.
- That it is suitable for **adolescents with finger-joint pain**, **post-surgical rehab** (beyond following the surgeon's plan),
  **FDP avulsions**, fractures, dislocations, unstable joints, infections or neurovascular symptoms.
- That it **replaces** a doctor, hand surgeon, hand therapist or physiotherapist.
